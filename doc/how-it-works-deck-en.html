The problem

"Made in Canada" relies on the supplier saying so.

The Buy Canadian Policy gives Canadian-made goods preference in defence procurement. But today, the labels — Made in Canada / Product of Canada — depend on supplier self-reporting. By the time a drone reaches the Canadian Armed Forces, its parts have passed through five companies, three countries, and four accounting systems. Nobody can reconstruct it.

The building blocks

Three ideas. Everything else is built from these.

If you understand the next three slides, you understand the whole system. No more jargon required.

Block 1 The fingerprint

A way to turn any document into a 64-character tag. Change one comma, the tag changes completely.

Block 2 The personal seal

A private stamp only you have, and a public photo of the stamp anyone can use to verify it. (Public + private key.)

Block 3 The signed claim

A small structured record describing one supplier's work, sealed with their private key. (An "attestation.")

04
Building block 1 · the fingerprint

Any document → a short tag. Change one comma → different tag.

Document A: "Apex made 1 airframe, cost $412"
→
sha256:9c1f8b2e…e3a7
— change one digit —
Document A': "Apex made 1 airframe, cost $413"
→
sha256:71d440aa…22c1

A fingerprinting machine (formally: a hash function) takes any document, of any size, and returns a short tag like sha256:9c1f…e3a7.

01Same document → same tag. Every computer in the world agrees on the result.
02One byte different → completely different tag. Tampering is instantly visible.
03You can't run it backwards. The tag reveals nothing about the original document.
04The tag is the document's identity. If we sign the tag, we've effectively signed the document.
05
Building block 2 · the personal seal

The private stamp you keep. The public photo anyone can use to check it.

Private key
The physical stamp. Locked in a safe — or, for serious suppliers, on a hardware device nobody can copy. Only the supplier can press it.
— mathematically paired —
Public key
A photograph of what the seal looks like. Published openly. Anyone in the world holds a copy and can use it to check.

Each supplier holds a mathematically-paired key. The private half they keep. The public half they publish.

01Signing. The supplier presses their private stamp on a document's fingerprint. The result is a "signature" — proof this exact document was sealed by this exact stamp.
02Verifying. Anyone with the public photograph can confirm the signature is real — without ever holding the private stamp.
03Not the same as hashing. Signing is not "hash the document plus the key." Two different keys do two different jobs: private creates the signature, public verifies it. The public key can't forge — only check.
04The headline guarantee. You can prove the supplier signed it without ever seeing their private key. Tampering and forgery are mathematically detectable.
06
Building block 3 · the signed claim

One supplier contribution = one attestation.

Apex Machining — Attestation
issuer: "Apex Machining, Mississauga ON"
activity: "machine drone airframe"
inputs: [sha256:b2c4…91]
labour_CA: $412.00
materials_CA: $38.50
labour_foreign: $0.00
location: "Mississauga, ON, Canada"
timestamp: "2026-05-20T14:33Z"
signed: Ed25519 · Apex private key · base64…

An attestation is one small structured record — a few lines of text — describing one supplier's contribution. Same shape every time, so a computer can read it.

→Who did the work (the issuer, with a verified identity)
→What they did (extract, manufacture, assemble, integrate…)
→What they consumed (fingerprints of upstream attestations — the links in the chain)
→What it cost, where (Canadian labour, foreign labour, Canadian materials, foreign materials)
→Where it happened (the audited facility)
→A signature proving the supplier authored it

08
The workflow · part 1

How one supplier creates a signed claim.

1 Supplier fills in the claim Either by typing JSON, or — much more likely — typing plain English like "we milled 50 airframes, 3 machinists × 6 hrs at $42/hr". An AI assistant drafts the structured attestation from the plain-English input.
2 Supplier reviews and edits The human checks every field — costs, location, inputs. This is the accountability step. The AI proposed; the human disposes.
3 The claim's fingerprint is computed The clean structured text gets hashed → sha256:9c1f…e3a7. A 64-character identity.
4 Hardware signs the fingerprint The supplier presses their private key (on a YubiKey or HSM) onto the fingerprint. Out pops a signature.
5 Claim + signature → sent out The signed attestation joins the chain. The supplier's private key never leaves their device.
09
The workflow · part 2

How anyone — without permission — verifies it.

The verifier could be a procurement officer, an auditor, a journalist, or a rival supplier. They don't ask anyone's permission. They just check the math.

1 Look up the supplier's public key From the official registry of accredited Canadian suppliers. If the supplier isn't on it → reject immediately as UNKNOWN_ISSUER.
2 Re-compute the claim's fingerprint Take the claim text, hash it again. Should match the fingerprint that was originally signed.
3 Check the signature Math says either: yes, the holder of the matching private key produced this signature — or no, they didn't. No grey zone. Failure → SIGNATURE_INVALID.
4 Check structural integrity No replayed attestations, no broken links to upstream, no cycles, no duplicate IDs. Every check has a named failure reason.

Each supplier signs their attestation, and includes the fingerprints of the upstream attestations they consumed. Change any node → its fingerprint changes → every downstream link now points to nothing. No silent edits possible. No blockchain required.

The workflow · part 4

The math: walk the tree, sum the costs, apply the law.

Total cost of the drone (all stages summed)   =   $1,797.60
Canadian portion (all stages summed)   =   $1,418.60
Canadian content = 1418.60 / 1797.60 × 100 = 78.9%
If < 51% Neither label Doesn't qualify under Canadian law.
If ≥ 51% & final assembly in Canada Made in Canada Our drone: 78.9% ≥ 51% ✓   assembled in Ottawa ✓
If ≥ 98% & final assembly in Canada Product of Canada Our drone: 78.9% < 98% ✗ — held down by foreign motors.
This computation is pure arithmetic — same input, same answer, every time. Reproducible by any third party. No AI ever touches this path.

Where AI goes — and where it doesn't

Cryptography for integrity. AI for plausibility.

Fixed math — no AI

The verdict path

  • Signing attestations (hardware + human)
  • Verifying signatures and fingerprints
  • Checking the registry of accredited suppliers
  • Walking the tree and summing costs
  • Computing the percentage
  • Applying the 98% / 51% legal thresholds
AI helper — advisory only

Beside the path

  • Drafting attestations from plain English / invoices
  • Flagging anomalies (labour cost 5× the regional norm)
  • Reconciling declared inputs vs. shipping manifests
  • Answering questions ("which drones have foreign motors?")
  • Spotting wash rings across the supplier graph
Four things AI never does: sign attestations · compute the legal verdict · be the source of truth for any factual claim · participate in the cryptographic verification path.

The demo · live attacks on stage

Three ways to cheat. Three rejections.

Tamper01
Secretly edit the imported chip's cost from $130 down to $13, trying to lift the Canadian percentage.
SIGNATURE_INVALID — fingerprint of the edited claim no longer matches the original signature
Forge02
Invent a fake "Canadian" supplier and sign a fake attestation with a key you generated yourself.
UNKNOWN_ISSUER — your public key isn't on the accreditation registry
Replay03
Paste a real, validly-signed attestation from another product onto this product's chain.
REPLAY_DETECTED — product ID inside the signed bytes doesn't match this product
Three attacks, three rejections, and the Canadian-content percentage never moves — because the math only runs on attestations that pass every check. This is the highest-scoring 45 seconds of the whole demo.

Why this matters
From "trust me" to "check the math."
Today, Buy Canadian is enforced by supplier self-reporting. Fraud is easy and deniable. With cryptographic attestations, every claim is independently checkable, every change leaves a trace, every fake supplier is rejected at the gate, and every verdict is reproducible. We don't eliminate fraud — but we make it expensive and traceable instead of cheap and invisible.
